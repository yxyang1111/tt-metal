%%  ABSTRACT
%% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}

Multi-Latent Attention (MLA), introduced in the DeepSeek-V2/V3
architecture, compresses key-value caches into a low-rank latent space,
dramatically reducing memory footprint.  During decode, however, MLA
must online-restore per-head K and V, creating a
computation--data-movement pattern fundamentally different from
conventional multi-head attention (MHA) and grouped-query attention
(GQA).  On spatial accelerators---many-core substrates with distributed
scratchpad SRAM, decoupled data movers, and hardware multicast over the
on-chip network---this mapping problem makes \emph{dataflow design} the
decisive performance factor.

We present \sysname, a framework for analyzing and exploring MLA
dataflows on spatial accelerators.  Using Tenstorrent's Tensix
architecture as the concrete substrate, we systematically characterize
three core mapping challenges: (i)~complex data reuse arising from the
interaction between distributed SRAM, NoC multicast, shared-latent
representations, and per-head restoration, (ii)~pipeline coupling in
decoupled reader--compute--writer execution that intensifies with
sequence length, and (iii)~a large, non-obvious design space whose
optimal configurations shift across regimes.  Guided by these findings,
\sysname introduces a parameterized dataflow design, a cost model with
second-order empirical corrections for non-ideal effects, an \autoTuner{}
for efficient design space exploration, and architecture-level
exploration.

Evaluation on Tenstorrent Wormhole hardware reveals a clear
\emph{\phaseTransition} in the decode bottleneck as sequence length
increases: from compute-critical at short sequences to
reader--writer saturated coupling at 32K tokens.  Fine-grained profiling
decomposes this transition at the sub-component level, providing
actionable optimization targets.
Architecture simulation further
identifies non-obvious design trade-offs where MLA diverges from MHA
in optimal architecture selection.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

%% P1: Attention is important and bottlenecked in decode
Attention mechanisms, the key computational primitive of
transformer-based models, increasingly dominate autoregressive large
language model (LLM) inference during the decode phase.  For each
generated token, attention requires the query to access the entire
key-value (KV) cache, giving decode an arithmetic intensity of
$\mathcal{O}(1)$ and making it strongly memory-bandwidth
bound~\cite{pope2023efficiently}.  As context lengths grow from
thousands to tens of thousands of tokens, attention over the expanding
KV cache drives both capacity and bandwidth pressure, and decode
latency often determines end-to-end serving
performance~\cite{kwon2023pagedattention, zhong2024distserve}.

%% P2: MLA reduces KV storage but changes the operator
However, the structure of the attention operator itself is also
changing.  Multi-head attention (MHA)~\cite{vaswani2017attention}
stores independent KV heads and therefore incurs large cache
footprints, while grouped-query attention (GQA)~\cite{ainslie2023gqa}
and multi-query attention (MQA)~\cite{shazeer2019mqa} reduce KV head
count to lower memory cost.  Multi-Latent Attention (MLA), introduced
in DeepSeek-V2~\cite{deepseekai2024deepseekv2} and
DeepSeek-V3~\cite{deepseekai2024deepseekv3}, goes further by
compressing per-token KV state into a low-rank latent space, reducing
the cache footprint from $2Hd$ to $d_c$ elements.  Yet MLA does not
simply make decode smaller: it must \emph{online-restore} per-head K
and V through learned projections, creating a restore-and-attend
workflow whose compute and data-movement behavior differs qualitatively
from MHA/GQA.  The central question is therefore not only how much KV
storage MLA saves, but how this new computation should be organized
efficiently on real hardware.

%% P3: Spatial accelerators make dataflow the main problem
That question is especially consequential on spatial
accelerators---many-core architectures with per-core scratchpad SRAM,
decoupled data-mover engines, and hardware-supported multicast over the
on-chip network---which offer a promising substrate for attention
acceleration~\cite{tenstorrent2024wormhole, tandem2023,
dynamic-alloc2024}.  Unlike GPU-centric optimizations that focus
primarily on warp-level tiling, spatial accelerators require explicit
choices about \emph{where} data is placed, \emph{when} it is moved, and
\emph{how} cores cooperate.  For MLA, these choices determine whether
latent sharing, projection work, and attention computation remain
balanced or become bottlenecked by communication and buffering.  As a
result, prior mappings developed for simpler decode kernels are not
directly suited to MLA, and mapping MLA onto a spatial accelerator is
fundamentally a \emph{dataflow design} problem.

%% P4: Three coupled challenges motivate our work
We identify three coupled challenges in this mapping problem:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Complex data reuse.}  MLA exposes reuse patterns that do
    not exist in conventional MHA: the latent cache is shared across
    heads, but the subsequent per-head restoration couples placement,
    buffering, and communication decisions across spatial, temporal,
    and topological dimensions.  As a result, MLA decode lies in a
    mixed compute--memory regime whose balance shifts with sequence
    length.
  \item \textbf{Pipeline coupling in decoupled execution.}
    Compared with MHA/GQA decode, MLA inserts an additional
    online-restoration stage into the reader--compute--writer pipeline.
    Although spatial accelerators rely on decoupled execution to hide
    latency, the stages remain coupled in practice through shared
    buffers, back-pressure, and NoC contention, effects not captured by
    first-order analytical models~\cite{parashar2019timeloop,
    kwon2019maestro}.
  \item \textbf{Large, non-obvious design space.}  The resulting
    dataflow space---spanning head grouping, tile grouping, block size,
    pipeline depth, and reduction topology---contains hundreds of
    feasible configurations whose relative performance shifts across
    sequence-length regimes, making manual tuning infeasible.
\end{enumerate}

%% P5: Our response
This paper fundamentally tackles these challenges with \sysname
(\brandexpansion), a framework for analyzing and exploring MLA
dataflows on spatial accelerators.  We first characterize MLA's reuse
structure and pipeline-coupling behavior through quantitative analysis
and hardware measurements (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}).  We then
derive a parameterized dataflow concept and implementation that expose
the key spatial, temporal, and topological design trade-offs
(Sections~\ref{sec:concept}--\ref{sec:design}).  Finally, we introduce
a cost-model-guided DSE workflow with empirical calibration, automated
search, and architecture-level exploration
(Sections~\ref{sec:costmodel}--\ref{sec:arch-explore}).

%% P6: Contributions
We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A systematic characterization of MLA's core mapping
    challenges on spatial accelerators,} showing how shared-latent
    reuse, compute--memory balance, and pipeline coupling evolve with
    sequence length and differ from conventional MHA decode
    (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}).

  \item \textbf{A parameterized MLA dataflow concept and design} that
    leverages multicast and reduction primitives to exploit MLA's
    shared-latent structure while making the key spatial, temporal, and
    topological trade-offs explicit
    (Sections~\ref{sec:concept}--\ref{sec:design}).

  \item \textbf{A DSE framework with a second-order cost model and an
    \autoTuner{},} capturing non-ideal effects such as reader
    back-pressure, writer stalls, and multicast injection hotspots
    while efficiently navigating the large design space
    (Section~\ref{sec:costmodel}).

  \item \textbf{Hardware validation and architecture exploration} that
    reveal a bottleneck \emph{\phaseTransition} in decode, from
    compute-critical to reader--writer-saturated execution, and
    identify divergent optimal configurations for MLA versus MHA
    (Sections~\ref{sec:eval}--\ref{sec:arch-explore}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

We establish the two foundations required for the remainder of the
paper: the operator semantics of MLA and FlashMLA-style decode
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-mla}), and the architectural primitives of
Tenstorrent's spatial accelerator that determine feasible dataflow
strategies (Section~\ref{sec:bg-arch}).


%%% ----- 2.1 MLA / FlashMLA -----

\subsection{Multi-Latent Attention and FlashMLA Decode}\label{sec:bg-mla}

\paragraph{Conventional decode and the KV cache.}
Autoregressive decode generates one output token per step by attending
over the full token history.  The attention for head $h$ is:
\begin{equation}
  o_h = \mathrm{softmax}\!\Bigl(
    \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,q_h K_h^\top + M\Bigr)\, V_h,
  \qquad K_h, V_h \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d},
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the current context length and $M$ is a causal mask.
Because the new query accesses the \emph{full} key-value (KV) cache at
each step, decode has arithmetic intensity $\mathcal{O}(1)$~FLOP/Byte
and is strongly memory-bandwidth
bound~\cite{pope2023efficiently}.  GQA~\cite{ainslie2023gqa} and
MQA~\cite{shazeer2019mqa} reduce head count but retain the per-head KV
structure.

\paragraph{Multi-Latent Attention.}
MLA~\cite{deepseekai2024deepseekv2, deepseekai2024deepseekv3}
eliminates per-head KV storage by projecting each token's key--value
state into a shared low-rank latent $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c}$, with
$d_c \ll 2Hd$.  A separate RoPE branch~\cite{su2024roformer} retains
explicit positional information.  During decode, head-$h$ keys and
values are on-the-fly reconstructed via learned up-projections
$W_{UK}^{(h)}, W_{UV}^{(h)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c \times d}$.  The
result is a per-token cache footprint of $\mathcal{O}(d_c)$ instead of
$\mathcal{O}(2Hd)$; for DeepSeek-V2/V3 ($H{=}128$, $d{=}128$,
$d_c{=}512$), this is a $64\times$ reduction.  The key architectural
consequence is that decode is no longer a direct cache lookup but a
\emph{restore-and-attend} operator, whose compute and data-movement
structure differs qualitatively from MHA and GQA.

\paragraph{FlashMLA: a unified-cache view.}
The FlashMLA-style decode kernel studied in this
paper~\cite{flashmla2024gpu} implements MLA by representing the cached
state as a single unified tensor.  After query-side projection, the
kernel receives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flashmla-kernel}
  \begin{aligned}
    Q_{\mathrm{attn}} &= \bigl[Q_{\mathrm{latent}} \mid Q_{\mathrm{rope}}\bigr]
      \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times (d_c + d_r)}, \\
    K_{\mathrm{cache}} &= \bigl[C \mid K_{\mathrm{rope}}\bigr]
      \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times (d_c + d_r)},
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and computes exact attention using the full cache width for score
accumulation while treating the leading $d_v{=}d_c$ columns as the
value view:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flashmla-attn}
  O = \mathrm{softmax}\!\bigl(
    \alpha\, Q_{\mathrm{attn}} K_{\mathrm{cache}}^\top + M
  \bigr)\, K_{\mathrm{cache}}[\,:,\!:\!d_v].
\end{equation}
For the DeepSeek-V2/V3 configuration, $d_c{=}512$ and $d_r{=}64$,
giving a cache width of 576 with a 512-wide value view.  This asymmetry
between the score computation width ($d_c+d_r$) and the output width
($d_v$) is a key source of tile-packing and DRAM-traffic constraints
examined in Section~\ref{sec:challenges}.

Flash-style chunked execution~\cite{dao2022flashattention} processes
$K_{\mathrm{cache}}$ in sequence blocks $\Bkv$ and maintains only the
running online-softmax state $(m, \ell, o)$ in L1, avoiding
materialization of the full $1 \times L$ score
matrix~\cite{flashmla2024gpu}.  Two properties of
Equation~\eqref{eq:flashmla-attn} are central to this paper's
contributions.  First, the latent cache is \emph{shared across all
heads}, creating cross-head data reuse that conventional decode entirely
lacks.  Second, the decode computation combines a sequence-length-linear
DRAM read, per-head projection work, and a reduction over blocks---a
mixed compute--memory regime whose balance depends on dataflow choices
rather than fixed kernel parameters.

\subsection{Tenstorrent Spatial Accelerator}\label{sec:bg-arch}

Tenstorrent's Wormhole and Blackhole are many-core spatial
accelerators~\cite{tenstorrent2024wormhole} organized as a 2-D mesh of
Tensix cores with distributed scratchpad SRAM, explicitly managed data
movement, and hardware-assisted collective communication.
Table~\ref{tab:hw-params} summarizes key parameters.  We use
\emph{spatial accelerator} to denote this class of architectures
characterized by many processing elements, per-PE local memory, and
explicit inter-PE communication over an on-chip network.

\paragraph{Tensix core microarchitecture.}
Each Tensix core contains approximately 1.5\,MB of L1 SRAM partitioned
into software-managed circular buffers (CBs), plus three classes of
lightweight controllers operating concurrently.  NCRISC drives
DMA-style DRAM-to-L1 transfers; BRISC orchestrates L1-to-L1 unicast or
multicast injections and result write-back; three TRISCs execute the
unpack--math--pack compute pipeline.  Stages synchronize through CB
slot reservations and release notifications: the reader (NCRISC) must
reserve a free slot before issuing a DMA, the compute stage (TRISC)
blocks on \texttt{cb\_wait\_front} until data arrives, and the writer
(BRISC) stalls until compute produces valid output.  This decoupled
architecture allows DRAM latency to be hidden under compute in the
common case, but creates bidirectional \emph{back-pressure coupling}
when any stage falls behind---a behavior we quantify in
Section~\ref{sec:challenge-coupling}.

\paragraph{On-chip network and collective communication.}
Two independent 2-D mesh NoCs serve unicast and multicast traffic
independently, enabling full-duplex data movement.  A sender core
addresses a rectangular destination region in a single transaction;
the NoC replicates the payload internally without redundant DRAM
reads.  This hardware multicast primitive is the cornerstone of
\sysname's shared-latent distribution strategy
(Section~\ref{sec:concept-mcast}): one DRAM read of a latent block is
broadcast to all cores in a worker group processing distinct attention
heads, eliminating $\mathcal{O}(H)$~DRAM reads and replacing them with
$\mathcal{O}(1)$ DRAM read plus one NoC multicast.

\paragraph{DRAM banking and placement.}
DRAM controllers are distributed along the chip boundary and mapped to
contiguous NoC rows.  The physical proximity of a worker group to its
DRAM banks directly affects read latency and available bandwidth.  An
MLA mapping must therefore jointly optimize (i)~the rectangular shape
of the worker group for multicast efficiency, (ii)~its placement for
bank affinity, and (iii)~the reduction topology for partial-output
aggregation.  These dimensions are coupled: changing the worker-group
shape alters both the multicast fanout and the set of reachable DRAM
banks.  Section~\ref{sec:design} formalizes this coupling through the
\SblockName{} abstraction.

\paragraph{Dataflow terminology.}
Following Eyeriss\,v2~\cite{chen2019eyerissv2} and
FlatAttention~\cite{flatattention2024}, we use \emph{dataflow} to
refer to the algorithmic organization of tensor tiling, compute order,
spatial parallelism, and collective communication, and \emph{mapping}
for its concrete instantiation on hardware: the assignment of tensor
shards to DRAM banks, CB slots, worker groups, and NoC paths.

\section{Challenges with MLA on Spatial Accelerators}\label{sec:challenges}

In this section, we characterize the three core mapping challenges
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}: complex data reuse, pipeline
coupling in decoupled execution, and a large, non-obvious design
space.  Together, these challenges explain why MLA requires a distinct
dataflow design methodology on spatial accelerators and motivate the
design decisions in Sections~\ref{sec:concept}--\ref{sec:design}.


%%% ----- 3.1 Challenge 1: Complex Data Reuse -----

\subsection{Challenge 1: Complex Data Reuse}\label{sec:challenge-reuse}

%% Following FLAT §3.1's style: quantitative analysis of operational
%% intensity, showing why existing approaches are insufficient.

We first show that \sharedLatentMulticast{} and per-head restoration
place MLA decode in a compute--memory regime whose effective balance
depends on the chosen mapping.

\paragraph{MHA decode: purely memory-bound.}
In standard MHA decode, the per-head operational intensity is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oi-mha}
  I_{\text{MHA}} = \frac{2Ld}{2Ld \cdot b} = \frac{1}{b} ,
\end{equation}
where $b$ is element size in bytes.  For BF16 ($b{=}2$),
$I_{\text{MHA}} = 0.5$~FLOP/Byte---deeply memory-bound on any
accelerator.  Each head's KV data is independent, and no cross-head
reuse exists.

\paragraph{MLA decode: a mixed regime.}
MLA introduces two qualitative differences.  First, the latent cache
$C \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_c}$ is \emph{shared across all $H$
heads}.  On a spatial accelerator, this enables reading $C$ once
from DRAM and multicasting to cores processing different heads,
reducing DRAM traffic from $\mathcal{O}(H)$ reads to $\mathcal{O}(1)$
per latent block---an advantage that standard MHA entirely lacks.

Second, MLA adds per-head up-projections ($W_{UK}^{(h)}, W_{UV}^{(h)}$)
that transform the $d_c$-dimensional latent to $d$-dimensional K/V.
The operational intensity of the \emph{combined} latent-read +
projection + attention becomes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oi-mla}
  I_{\text{MLA}} \approx \frac{2Ld + 2Ld_c d / \Ghead}{L d_c \cdot b / N_{\text{mcast}} + L d \cdot b},
\end{equation}
where $\Ghead$ is the number of heads grouped per core and
$N_{\text{mcast}}$ is the multicast fanout.  The numerator includes
both attention FLOPs ($2Ld$) and projection FLOPs ($2Ld_c d/\Ghead$);
the denominator reflects DRAM reads amortized by multicast and local
output writes.  For typical DeepSeek-V2 parameters ($d{=}128$,
$d_c{=}512$, $H{=}32$), $I_{\text{MLA}}$ ranges from $\sim$2 to
$\sim$30~FLOP/Byte depending on $\Ghead$ and $N_{\text{mcast}}$,
placing MLA decode in a mixed \emph{compute--memory regime} rather than
a purely memory-bound or purely compute-bound one.

\paragraph{Implication.}
This mixed regime means that MLA's performance is sensitive to the
\emph{balance} among DRAM bandwidth, multicast efficiency, and
per-core compute throughput.  Dataflow techniques designed for purely
memory-bound MHA decode (e.g., Flash-Decoding~\cite{flashdecoding2023})
or purely compute-bound prefill (e.g.,
FlashAttention~\cite{dao2022flashattention}) are ill-suited.
Unlike the activation-activation operators (L, A) analyzed in
FLAT~\cite{kao2023flat}, whose low operational intensity is
independent of batch size, MLA's effective intensity is a function of
dataflow parameters ($\Ghead$, $N_{\text{mcast}}$), making dataflow
design the \emph{primary lever} for performance.


%%% ----- 3.2 Challenge 2: Pipeline Coupling -----

\subsection{Challenge 2: Pipeline Coupling in Decoupled Execution}\label{sec:challenge-coupling}

%% Following FLAT §3.2 style: identifying a structural challenge
%% that existing frameworks don't address.

Spatial accelerators like Tenstorrent's Tensix employ decoupled
reader--compute--writer pipelines connected by circular buffers,
enabling overlap of data movement and computation.  A first-order
analysis would predict that the steady-state throughput is
$\max(t_{\text{read}}, t_{\text{compute}}, t_{\text{write}})$ per block.

\paragraph{Coupling grows with sequence length.}
In practice, we observe from hardware profiling
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-profile}) that pipeline stages exhibit
increasing \emph{mutual coupling} as sequence length grows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{reader} (NCRISC) stalls on CB reserve when
    downstream compute cannot consume K tiles fast enough.  At
    $L{=}$1K, reserve stalls account for $<$1\% of reader time;
    at $L{=}$32K, they dominate at $>$60\%.
  \item The \textbf{writer} (BRISC) stalls waiting for local compute
    to produce partial outputs ($\sim$50\%) and for reduction partners
    to complete ($\sim$50\%), regardless of sequence length.
  \item The \textbf{compute} (TRISC) transitions from being purely
    \emph{input-starved} (87\% wait-front at $L{=}$256) to being
    \emph{squeezed from both ends} (29\% reserve-back at $L{=}$32K).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why first-order models fail.}
Existing dataflow mapping frameworks~\cite{parashar2019timeloop,
kwon2019maestro} assume independent pipeline stages
and do not model these coupling effects.  The resulting predictions
are optimistic: as we show in Section~\ref{sec:eval-accuracy},
first-order models underestimate latency by up to $1.8\times$ at
long sequences.  \emph{Accurate cost modeling requires second-order
empirical terms that capture cross-stage back-pressure.}

\paragraph{Comparison with tile-based accelerator analyses.}
FlatAttention~\cite{flatattention2024} models multicast and
reduction latencies on tile-based architectures but assumes
synchronous execution within each pipeline stage.  Our analysis
reveals that the \emph{asynchronous coupling} between stages---specific
to decoupled architectures like Tensix---is a first-order effect that
must be modeled explicitly.


%%% ----- 3.3 Challenge 3: Large Design Space -----

\subsection{Challenge 3: Large, Non-Obvious Design Space}\label{sec:challenge-space}

%% Following FLAT §3.3 style: showing why the problem cannot be
%% solved by simple heuristics.

The difficulty is not just the number of parameters, but the fact that
the best choices depend jointly on computation, communication, and
buffering behavior across operating regimes.

The MLA dataflow design space on a spatial accelerator is governed by
multiple interacting parameters: head grouping ($\Ghead$), tile
grouping ($\Tgroup$), KV block size ($\Bkv$), pipeline depth,
overlap mode, reduction topology, and page size.  After constraint
filtering, 300--800 feasible configurations remain.

\paragraph{Optimal configuration shifts with operating regime.}
The key difficulty is that the optimal configuration is \emph{not
stable} across sequence lengths.  At short sequences ($L \leq$ 1K),
compute is the bottleneck and configurations maximizing per-core FLOPs
(high $\Ghead$, small $\Tgroup$) are preferred.  At long sequences
($L \geq$ 16K), reader--writer coupling dominates, and configurations
that reduce DRAM traffic (large $N_{\text{mcast}}$, deep pipeline) are
preferred.  This regime shift means no single configuration is
universally optimal.

\paragraph{Comparison with existing DSE approaches.}
FLAT~\cite{kao2023flat} addresses a similar map-space exploration
problem for L-A fusion, but its design space is defined over
intra-operator tiling and compute order for a \emph{single PE}.
Our design space additionally spans \emph{inter-core} dimensions
(multicast topology, reduction tree, \SblockName{} structure) unique to
spatial accelerators.  FlatAttention~\cite{flatattention2024}
explores group size ($G_x \times G_y$) as a parameter but does not
address MLA-specific dimensions or provide automated DSE.

\paragraph{Implication.}
Manual tuning or single-point optimization is insufficient.
An \emph{automated design space exploration} framework with an
accurate cost model is necessary to navigate this space efficiently.

\section{\brand Dataflow Concept}\label{sec:concept}

Guided by the challenges identified above, we present the high-level
design concept of \sysname, focusing on \emph{why} specific design
decisions are made before detailing \emph{how} they are implemented
(Section~\ref{sec:design}).


%%% ----- 4.1 Exploiting Shared-Latent Structure -----

\subsection{Exploiting \SharedLatentMulticast}\label{sec:concept-mcast}

%% Like FLAT §4.1: identifying the fusion opportunity
%% Here: identifying the multicast opportunity unique to MLA

\paragraph{MHA vs.\ MLA: cross-head data sharing.}
In MHA, each head maintains independent K/V tensors.  On a spatial
accelerator with $P$ cores, if each core processes a distinct head,
each must independently read its own KV from DRAM---no cross-head
sharing is possible.  The total DRAM KV traffic scales as $\mathcal{O}(P \cdot Ld \cdot b)$.

MLA's latent cache $C$ is shared across all heads.  When $P$ cores
process different heads, a single DRAM read of a latent block can be
\emph{multicast} to all $P$ cores via the on-chip network, reducing
DRAM traffic to $\mathcal{O}(L d_c \cdot b)$---a
$\frac{P \cdot d}{d_c}$ fold reduction.  This is analogous to
FlatAttention's~\cite{flatattention2024} use of row-wise multicast for
Q blocks and column-wise multicast for K/V blocks, but with a crucial
difference: in MLA, the \emph{same} data (latent $C$) serves
\emph{all} heads, whereas in FlatAttention's MHA mapping, K/V is
already head-specific.

\paragraph{Design decision.}
We organize cores into \emph{\SblockNames} where a designated
\emph{sender} core reads latent pages from DRAM and multicasts to
\emph{receiver} cores via hardware multicast on NoC\,0.  The multicast
fanout ($C_s - 1$ receivers per \SblockName) directly translates to DRAM
traffic reduction.


%%% ----- 4.2 Managing the Projection--Attention Pipeline -----

\subsection{Managing the Projection--Attention Pipeline}\label{sec:concept-pipeline}

%% Like FLAT §4.2: identifying challenges with the fusion/pipeline

\paragraph{The added projection stage.}
Unlike MHA where received K/V data is immediately consumed by the
$QK^\top$ matmul, MLA requires a per-head up-projection
($C \rightarrow K_h, V_h$) before attention.  This creates a
three-stage \emph{read → project → attend} pipeline within each core,
increasing the depth of the computation chain and raising the
sensitivity to circular-buffer depth.

\paragraph{Design decision.}
We leverage the decoupled pipeline of Tensix: NCRISC pre-fetches
latent blocks into a CB of depth $D$; TRISC performs projection and
attention in a fused kernel; BRISC handles output distribution.  The
pipeline depth $D$ is a key design parameter: deeper pipelines hide
read latency but consume more L1 SRAM.  As shown in
Section~\ref{sec:challenge-coupling}, the \emph{coupling} between
these stages makes $D$ a critical performance lever.


%%% ----- 4.3 Reduction Topology -----

\subsection{Aggregating Partial Results via On-Chip Reduction}\label{sec:concept-reduce}

%% Like FLAT's discussion of managing intermediate tensor dependencies.
%% Here: managing partial attention statistics across cores.

When multiple \SblockNames{} process different chunks of the KV sequence for
the same head, their partial attention outputs $(o, m, s)$ must be
\emph{reduced} to produce the final output.  This is analogous to
FlatAttention's~\cite{flatattention2024} row-wise reduction of O
slices, but with a key difference: in MLA decode, the reduction spans
the \emph{sequence dimension} (across \SblockNames{}) rather than the
\emph{head dimension} (within a group).

\paragraph{Design decision.}
We employ a host-precomputed \emph{tree topology} on NoC\,1, where
BRISC drives semaphore-based send/receive protocols and TRISC
performs the numerical $\texttt{sdpa\_tail}$ merge at each tree level.
The tree topology balances reduction latency ($\mathcal{O}(\log N_s)$)
against NoC traffic, reflecting the broader insight that NoC
collective primitives are highly beneficial when properly co-designed
with the dataflow.

\section{\brand Dataflow Design}\label{sec:design}

This section details the concrete dataflow implementation, building
on the conceptual decisions from Section~\ref{sec:concept}.
We first present the end-to-end dataflow algorithm, then describe
the four-level tiling hierarchy, the placement strategy with DRAM
bank affinity, the communication primitives, and finally the
decoupled three-processor pipeline.


%%% ----- 5.1 End-to-End Dataflow -----

\subsection{End-to-End Dataflow}\label{sec:design-overview}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:dataflow} summarizes the complete MLA decode
dataflow.  The key design insight is that \emph{K movement is the
sole high-volume bottleneck}: V is a column-subset of K
($V = K[\,:\,,\,:d_v]$) and requires no separate transport, while Q
is small enough to broadcast once.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\brand Decode Dataflow}\label{alg:dataflow}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
\REQUIRE $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H_q \times d}$ (L1-sharded on output cores), \\
\hspace{1.15cm} $K \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times d}$ (ND-sharded in DRAM), $\mathrm{pos}$
\ENSURE $O \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H_q \times d_v}$
\STATE Partition cores into $N$ \SblockNames{} $\{S_1,\ldots,S_N\}$,
       each $M$ cores in rectangle $G_i$, bound to DRAM bank $b_i$
\STATE Chunk sequence: $C \leftarrow \lceil S/c_k \rceil$;
       assign \SblockName{} $i$ chunks $\{i, i{+}N, i{+}2N, \ldots\}$
       (stride $= N$, all on bank $b_i$)
\STATE \textbf{Q broadcast:} output core signals ``Q ready'';
       all workers pull $Q$ from output core L1
\FOR{each chunk $j$ assigned to this core (stride $N$)}
  \STATE \textbf{NCRISC:} read $K_j$ from DRAM $b_i$ into
         $\mathrm{CB}_K$ in $P$ pages via TRID pipeline
  \STATE \textbf{BRISC (sender):} multicast each page to $G_i$
         receivers as it completes (page-level pipeline with NCRISC)
  \STATE \textbf{TRISC:} flash attention chunk update in DEST:
  \STATE \quad $A_j \leftarrow Q \cdot K_j^\top$; \;
         apply mask if tail chunk
  \STATE \quad $m' \leftarrow \max(m,\;\mathrm{rowmax}(A_j))$; \;
         $\alpha \leftarrow \exp(m - m')$
  \STATE \quad $O \leftarrow \alpha \cdot O + \exp(A_j - m') \cdot V_j$
         \COMMENT{$V_j = K_j[\,:\,,\,:d_v]$}
  \STATE \quad $\ell \leftarrow \alpha \cdot \ell + \mathrm{rowsum}(\exp(A_j - m'))$; \;
         $m \leftarrow m'$
  \STATE Release $\mathrm{CB}_K$ (double-buffer swap)
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{Tree reduction} ($T = \lceil\log_2 N\rceil$ steps):
       sender writes $(O_i, m_i, \ell_i)$ to partner's $\mathrm{slot}_t$;
       receiver merges via online-softmax rescale
\STATE $O_{\mathrm{final}} \leftarrow O / \ell$ at output core
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


%%% ----- 5.2 Four-Level Tiling Hierarchy -----

\subsection{Four-Level Tiling Hierarchy}\label{sec:design-tiling}

The dataflow employs a four-level tiling strategy that maps
the sequence dimension from the full array down to per-tile
compute operations:

\paragraph{Level 1: Device chunk.}
In multi-device sequence parallelism, the full sequence $S$ is
partitioned across $D_{\mathrm{sp}}$ devices in units of
\emph{device chunks} of size $N \cdot c_k$ tokens.  Each device
owns a contiguous segment of the KV cache, sized to match the
total work of its $N$ \SblockNames.

\paragraph{Level 2: K chunk ($c_k$ tokens).}
Within a device, the local sequence is divided into $C = \lceil S / c_k \rceil$
chunks, each containing $c_k$ tokens (typically 128).
Chunks are assigned to \SblockNames{} via strided distribution:
\SblockName{} $i$ processes chunks $\{i, i{+}N, i{+}2N, \ldots\}$.
Because the KV cache is ND-sharded in DRAM with round-robin
bank assignment---chunk~$j$ resides on bank~$b_{j \bmod N}$---this
stride ensures that \emph{every chunk read by \SblockName{} $i$ falls on
its bound bank $b_i$}, eliminating cross-bank contention.

\paragraph{Level 3: Page ($p_k$ bytes).}
Each K chunk ($c_k \times d$ elements, typically ${\sim}$144\,KB in
BF16) exceeds the NoC single-packet limit ($\leq 8$\,KB on Wormhole,
$\leq 16$\,KB on Blackhole), so it is further divided into $P$
pages.  Pages are the granularity of both DRAM reads (multi-TRID
pipeline) and NoC multicast.  The page-level split enables
\emph{DRAM-read / multicast overlap}: BRISC can multicast page~$p$
while NCRISC reads page~$p{+}1$.

\paragraph{Level 4: Tile.}
K uses standard $32{\times}32$ tiles; Q uses $8{\times}32$
\emph{tiny tiles} (matching the per-core Q head count of 8 in
DeepSeek-V3).  The DEST register in TRISC holds the entire
flash-attention working state: $QK^\top$ scores, running max/sum
statistics, and partial $O$, requiring
$(d_v/32 + 2 + c_k/32) \times 16$ rows of DEST capacity.


%%% ----- 5.3 S-Block Placement -----

\subsection{\SblockName{} Placement and DRAM Bank Affinity}\label{sec:design-placement}

The placement strategy follows a \emph{\topologyFirst} principle:
we fix the spatial layout of \SblockNames{} before mapping data, rather
than deriving placement from tensor shapes.

\paragraph{DRAM bank affinity.}
Each \SblockName{} $S_i$ is bound to DRAM bank $b_i$ via the device API
\texttt{get\_optimal\_dram\_bank\_to\_logical\_worker\_assignment}.
The $M$ cores of $S_i$ are selected from the worker cores physically
nearest to $b_i$ on the NoC mesh, minimizing read latency and
hop count.

\paragraph{Multicast rectangle constraint.}
Hardware multicast on Tenstorrent requires destination cores to form
a \emph{physical-coordinate rectangle}.  Each \SblockName{}'s $M$ cores are
chosen to satisfy this constraint---e.g., a $2{\times}2$ rectangle on
Wormhole ($M{=}4$) or a $4{\times}2$ rectangle on Blackhole
($M{=}8$).  \SblockNames{} whose cores span the grid boundary exploit the
NoC's \emph{torus wraparound}: a multicast from row~9 to row~0
traverses a single hop on the torus topology.

\paragraph{Concrete layouts.}
On Wormhole~B0, six \SblockNames{} are placed in two groups of three on
the left and right halves of the $8{\times}7$ grid, bound to banks
$(1,2,0,4,9,8)$.
On Blackhole, eight \SblockNames{} span columns $0$--$3$ (left) and
$7$--$10$ (right) of the $11{\times}10$ grid, bound to banks
$(1,3,2,0,5,7,6,4)$.
Adjacent \SblockNames{} use alternating virtual channels
($\mathrm{VC} \in \{0,1\}$ for left, $\{2,3\}$ for right) to avoid
NoC link contention during concurrent DRAM reads.


%%% ----- 5.4 Communication Primitives -----

\subsection{Communication Primitives}\label{sec:design-comm}

The dataflow uses four distinct communication modes, each matched to
its data volume and spatial pattern:

\paragraph{(i) K DRAM read (NCRISC, NoC\,0, pull).}
Each \SblockName{}'s sender core reads K chunks from its bound DRAM bank.
NCRISC uses a sliding window of up to $W{=}14$ transaction IDs
(TRIDs) to pipeline page reads: pages are issued back-to-back, and
each completed page increments a shared counter $\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}$
visible to BRISC.  A double-buffered $\mathrm{CB}_K$ (capacity
$2 \times c_k \times d$) allows the current chunk's compute to
overlap with the next chunk's DRAM fetch.

\paragraph{(ii) K multicast (BRISC sender, NoC\,0, push).}
The sender's BRISC multicasts K data to the $M{-}1$ receivers within
rectangle $G_i$.  This operates at \emph{page granularity} in a
three-phase protocol:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Receiver ready}: each receiver's NCRISC signals
    $\sigma_{\mathrm{recv}} \mathrel{+}= 1$ after reserving
    $\mathrm{CB}_K$ space. Sender waits for $\sigma_{\mathrm{recv}} = M{-}1$.
  \item \emph{Page-level pipeline}: for each page $p$, sender waits
    $\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}} \geq p{+}1$ (NCRISC has read page $p$),
    then issues \texttt{noc\_write\_multicast} to $G_i$.
  \item \emph{Chunk complete}: after all $P$ pages, sender sets
    $\sigma_{\mathrm{mcast}} = \mathrm{VALID}$ via multicast semaphore;
    receivers push $\mathrm{CB}_K$ to notify TRISC.
\end{enumerate}
Hardware multicast replicates data at NoC branch points, consuming
bandwidth equivalent to a \emph{single} write regardless of receiver
count---a critical advantage over per-receiver unicast.

\paragraph{(iii) Q broadcast (BRISC, NoC\,0 signal + NoC\,1 data, pull).}
Q data (${\sim}$256\,B--1\,KB) is pre-sharded on the output core's
L1.  The broadcast uses an asymmetric signal-then-pull protocol:
all workers increment $\sigma_{\mathrm{q}}$ on the output core
(unicast, NoC\,1); the output core waits for all signals, then
multicasts a ``Q ready'' semaphore to the full-chip rectangle $G$
(NoC\,0); each worker individually reads Q from the output core's L1
(unicast, NoC\,1).  Data is \emph{not} multicast because workers are
non-rectangular; semaphore multicast is used because it carries only a
few bytes and benefits from full-chip coverage.

\paragraph{(iv) Tree reduction (BRISC, NoC\,1, push unicast).}
After local compute, partial results $(O_i, m_i, \ell_i)$ are
reduced across \SblockNames{} in $T = \lceil\log_2 N\rceil$ steps via a
host-precomputed binary tree.  At each step $t$, the sender writes
its partial output and statistics to the partner's buffer
$\mathrm{slot}_t$ using posted unicast writes on NoC\,1, then
increments bit~$t$ of $\sigma_{\mathrm{reduce}}$.
Per-step buffer slots prevent data corruption from out-of-order
arrivals (e.g., step~3 completing before step~2).
The receiver's TRISC performs the online-softmax merge:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail-merge}
  m'' = \max(m, m'), \quad
  O \leftarrow e^{m-m''}O + e^{m'-m''}O', \quad
  \ell \leftarrow e^{m-m''}\ell + e^{m'-m''}\ell'.
\end{equation}

Table~\ref{tab:comm-summary} summarizes the four modes and their
NoC assignments.

    \toprule
    \textbf{Primitive} & \textbf{NoC} & \textbf{Mode} &
      \textbf{Processor} & \textbf{Data/chunk} \\
    \midrule
    K DRAM read   & 0 & pull      & NCRISC & $c_k \!\times\! d \!\times\! b$ \\

%%% ----- 5.5 Decoupled Three-Processor Pipeline -----

\subsection{Decoupled Three-Processor Pipeline}\label{sec:design-pipeline}

The three RISC processors within each Tensix core execute
concurrently, forming a decoupled pipeline connected by circular
buffers and semaphores.
Their role assignment is driven by a single design principle:
\emph{separate DRAM access (NCRISC) from on-chip network control
(BRISC) so that they overlap at page granularity}.

\paragraph{NCRISC (data mover).}
Exclusively handles DRAM$\,\to\,$L1 transfers.  On sender cores, it
runs the multi-TRID sliding-window read and publishes page-completion
counts via $\sigma_{\mathrm{sync}}$.
On receiver cores, it signals readiness ($\sigma_{\mathrm{recv}}$)
and waits for the multicast-complete semaphore
($\sigma_{\mathrm{mcast}}$).

\paragraph{BRISC (network controller).}
Orchestrates all L1$\,\to\,$L1 communication: Q distribution,
K multicast (sender only), causal mask generation for tail chunks,
and tree-reduction send/receive.  BRISC's multiplexed role makes it
the most heavily loaded processor; profiling
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-writer}) confirms that BRISC
$\texttt{cb\_wait}$ accounts for $>$99\% of writer time at long
sequences.

\paragraph{TRISC (compute engine).}
Executes the entire flash-attention arithmetic in the DEST register
file: $QK^\top$ (mm1), online softmax update, $\mathrm{score} \times V$
(mm2, reading V as the first $d_v$ columns of K from $\mathrm{CB}_K$),
and---on reduction receivers---tail-reduction merges
(Equation~\ref{eq:tail-merge}).
No separate V circular buffer exists; V is consumed directly from the
K buffer via strided tile access, eliminating one entire data path
(DRAM read, multicast, CB, and synchronization).

\paragraph{Why this split?}
NCRISC's DRAM reads and BRISC's multicast writes both use NoC\,0 but
in \emph{opposite directions} (pull vs.\ push), enabling full
bidirectional utilization.  NoC\,1 is reserved for low-volume traffic
(Q pull, reduction unicast), avoiding contention with the K data
path.  The result is a three-stage pipeline where DRAM latency,
multicast latency, and compute latency can all overlap---subject to
the coupling effects analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:challenge-coupling}.

\section{Cost Model and DSE Framework}\label{sec:costmodel}


%%% ----- 6.1 First-Order Analytical Model -----

\subsection{First-Order Analytical Model}\label{sec:cm-first}

\paragraph{DRAM traffic.}
Under the per-core-read baseline, total DRAM bytes per decode step
for KV data is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dram-baseline}
  D_{\mathrm{base}} = P_{\mathrm{consumer}} \times 2Ld \cdot b .
\end{equation}
With multicast, only the sender reads from DRAM:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dram-mcast}
  D_{\mathrm{mcast}} = N_s \times 2Ld_c \cdot b + D_Q + D_O ,
\end{equation}
where $d_c$ is the latent dimension and $D_Q, D_O$ are the
(typically small) query and output traffic.  For MLA,
$d_c \ll Hd$, giving an additional reduction over MHA.

\paragraph{Per-block latencies.}
For a KV block of size $\Bkv$:
\begin{align}
  t_{\mathrm{dram}}  &= \Bkv \cdot d_c \cdot b \,/\, \mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{dram}} , \\
  t_{\mathrm{mcast}} &= \Bkv \cdot d_c \cdot b \,/\, \mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{noc}}
                        \cdot f_{\mathrm{hop}} , \\
  t_{\mathrm{comp}}  &= \Bkv \cdot d \cdot F \,/\, \mathrm{FLOPS}_{\mathrm{peak}} ,
\end{align}
where $f_{\mathrm{hop}}$ is a topology-dependent hop factor and
$F$ accounts for the FLOPs of both the latent projection and
attention arithmetic.

\paragraph{Steady-state and total latency.}
With pipeline depth $D$ and overlap enabled:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:steady}
  t_{\mathrm{steady}} = \max(t_{\mathrm{dram}},\; t_{\mathrm{mcast}},\;
    t_{\mathrm{comp}}) .
\end{equation}
Total latency:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total-lat}
  T = t_{\mathrm{startup}} + (N_{\mathrm{blk}} - 1) \cdot t_{\mathrm{steady}}
    + t_{\mathrm{reduce}} + t_{\mathrm{QO}} ,
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathrm{blk}} = \lceil L / \Bkv \rceil$, $t_{\mathrm{startup}}$
is the first-block latency, $t_{\mathrm{reduce}}$ is the tree-reduction
cost, and $t_{\mathrm{QO}}$ covers query/output overhead.


%%% ----- 6.2 Second-Order Empirical Model -----

\subsection{Second-Order Empirical Model}\label{sec:cm-second}

The first-order model assumes perfect pipeline overlap.  As identified
in Challenge~2 (Section~\ref{sec:challenge-coupling}), hardware
profiling reveals significant pipeline coupling effects that grow with
sequence length.  We augment the model with three empirical correction
terms:

\paragraph{Reader back-pressure ($\Delta_{\mathrm{rbp}}$).}
When TRISC cannot consume K tiles fast enough, NCRISC stalls on
$cb\_reserve\_back$.  We model:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{rbp}} = \beta_r \cdot L \cdot t_{\mathrm{comp}} / D ,
\end{equation}
where $\beta_r$ is a calibration coefficient and $D$ is pipeline depth.

\paragraph{Writer stall ($\Delta_{\mathrm{ws}}$).}
BRISC's $cb\_wait$ encompasses both sender wait and tree-child wait:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{ws}} = \beta_w \cdot (N_{\mathrm{blk}} \cdot t_{\mathrm{comp}}
    + N_{\mathrm{tree}} \cdot t_{\mathrm{merge}}) ,
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathrm{tree}}$ is the number of reduction steps.

\paragraph{Multicast injection hotspot ($\Delta_{\mathrm{mh}}$).}
When sender cores inject multicast traffic, NoC contention adds latency:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{mh}} = \beta_m \cdot N_{\mathrm{blk}} \cdot C_s \cdot b_{\mathrm{page}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Calibration.}
Coefficients $\beta_r, \beta_w, \beta_m$ are fit via ordinary least
squares on a small set of hardware measurements (typically 6--10
configurations across sequence lengths).


%%% ----- 6.3 Auto-Tuner -----

\subsection{\AutoTuner}\label{sec:cm-tuner}

%% Like FLAT §6's map-space exploration framework.

The \autoTuner{} mirrors the exhaustive map-space exploration approach
of FLAT~\cite{kao2023flat}, extended to MLA's spatial-accelerator
design space:
(1)~\textbf{Enumerate:} Generate all parameter combinations from
Table~\ref{tab:design-space}.
(2)~\textbf{Filter:} Prune infeasible configurations violating
SRAM capacity ($D \cdot \Bkv \cdot d_c \cdot \Ghead \cdot b \leq$
45\% of L1 per core) or core-count constraints.
(3)~\textbf{Score:} Evaluate each surviving configuration using the
cost model (first-order or calibrated second-order).
(4)~\textbf{Rank:} Sort by primary objective (latency) or produce
a Pareto front (latency vs.\ DRAM traffic).

Configurations are cached in a \emph{bucketed policy} indexed by
$\lceil \log_2 L \rceil$, enabling $\mathcal{O}(1)$ lookup at runtime.
For the typical design space of 300--800 feasible configurations,
the full search completes in $<$1\,s on a single CPU core.


%%% ----- 6.4 Architecture Simulation -----

\subsection{Architecture Simulation}\label{sec:cm-archsim}

To support architecture exploration (Section~\ref{sec:arch-explore}),
we extend the cost model to simulate alternative hardware configurations.

\paragraph{First-order simulation.}
Replace hardware constants ($\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{dram}}$,
$\mathrm{FLOPS}_{\mathrm{peak}}$, $\mathrm{BW}_{\mathrm{noc}}$,
L1 capacity) with target-architecture values and re-evaluate.

\paragraph{Second-order simulation.}
Inject the empirically-measured pipeline coupling ratios from
Wormhole into the target architecture's model.
This is motivated by the observation that coupling effects arise from
the \emph{structural} properties of the dataflow (CB depth, BRISC role
multiplexing, tree protocol granularity) rather than specific hardware
speeds, and thus transfer qualitatively across generations.

\section{Evaluation on Current Hardware}\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate \sysname through four questions:
(1)~what performance the current Wormhole implementation attains and

(2)~which microarchitectural utilization bottlenecks dominate across

(3)~how effectively the calibrated cost model and \autoTuner{} identify

(4)~what these findings imply for future architectures.
This section addresses the first three questions on current

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:eval-setup}

\textbf{Platform.}  Tenstorrent Wormhole N300 card with
$\sim$128 Tensix cores (post-harvest $\sim$112 usable), 12\,GB GDDR6,
$\sim$200\,GB/s aggregate DRAM bandwidth.

\textbf{Software.}  TT-Metalium runtime and TT-NN operator library;
Flash MLA implemented as a unified kernel
(\texttt{flash\_mla.hpp}) with Python-level operator
(\texttt{flash\_mla/op.py}).

\textbf{Workloads.}  MLA decode with DeepSeek-V2 parameters:
$H{=}32$, $d_c{=}512$, $d_{\mathrm{rope}}{=}64$, block size 64,
K chunk size 128.  Sequence lengths $L \in \{256, 512, 1\text{K},
2\text{K}, 4\text{K}, 8\text{K}, 16\text{K}, 32\text{K}\}$,
batch size $B{=}1$ for main results.

\textbf{Profiling.}  Two-pass methodology: (1)~latency runs
(2 warmup + 4 measurement iterations) for wall-time;
(2)~detailed Tracy/device-profiler runs for sub-component breakdown

\textbf{Baselines.}  We report the current TT-MLA implementation
(denoted \texttt{B0}) as the primary baseline, and use subsequent

%%% ----- 7.2 Performance Characterization -----

\subsection{Performance Characterization on Wormhole}\label{sec:eval-profile}

%% Like FLAT §7's compute utilization analysis across configurations.
%% Here: the phase transition is our central empirical finding.

We begin by characterizing the performance envelope of the current
FlashMLA implementation on Wormhole.  For the B0 baseline, measured
kernel latency is 74.2\,$\mu$s at 1K tokens, 171.6\,$\mu$s at 4K, and
1084.9\,$\mu$s at 32K.  These measurements further reveal a clear
\emph{\phaseTransition} in the decode bottleneck as sequence length
increases, directly validating Challenge~2
(Section~\ref{sec:challenge-coupling}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Short ($L \leq$ 1K): Compute-critical.}
    TRISC is the last to finish; reader and writer have slack.
    Kernel latency $\sim$41--88\,$\mu$s.

  \item \textbf{Medium (2K--8K): Writer approaching critical.}
    Writer $cb\_wait$ rises to 96--99\%; BRISC share approaches
    TRISC share.  Reader remains below critical.
    Latency $\sim$173--430\,$\mu$s.

  \item \textbf{Long (16K): Reader approaching critical.}
    NCRISC share reaches $\sim$89\%; reader begins competing
    with writer for critical-path status.
    Latency $\sim$720\,$\mu$s.

  \item \textbf{Very long (32K): Reader--writer saturated coupling.}
    All three threads operate near their window boundaries;
    the pipeline is deeply coupled with no single dominant bottleneck.
    Latency $\sim$1085\,$\mu$s.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, these results indicate that any substantial speedup at
long sequence lengths must arise primarily from mitigating
reader--writer coupling rather than from increasing arithmetic
throughput alone.  As quantified in Section~\ref{sec:eval-accuracy},
the gap between measured latency and an idealized first-order overlap
estimate reaches $\sim$1.8$\times$ at 32K, indicating substantial
remaining optimization opportunity under the current mapping.

%%% ----- 7.3 Microarchitectural Utilization Analysis -----

\subsection{Microarchitectural Utilization and Bottleneck Analysis}\label{sec:eval-utilization}

To explain these performance trends, we perform thread-level profiling
with custom markers to decompose execution time into reader, writer,
and compute activity, and to contrast decode against prefill.

%%% ----- 7.3.1 Reader Decomposition -----

\subsubsection{Reader Utilization and Stall Decomposition}\label{sec:eval-reader}

Fine-grained reader profiling decomposes NCRISC time into five
sub-stages: \emph{page-table lookup}, \emph{CB reserve}, \emph{DRAM
issue}, \emph{wait}, and \emph{CB push}.

The key result is a \emph{crossover} from issue-dominated
to reserve-dominated behavior:
\begin{itemize}
  \item At $L{=}$1K: \texttt{issue} accounts for 76.8\%;
    \texttt{reserve} is negligible (0.7\%).
  \item At $L{=}$32K: \texttt{reserve} dominates at 61.5\%;
    \texttt{issue} drops to 33.3\%.
\end{itemize}

K-path reserve accounts for $>$95\% of all reserve time (54.8\% of
total reader time at 32K), while V-path reserve remains $<$3\%.
This identifies \emph{K-path circular-buffer turnover}---limited by
downstream compute consumption---as the primary reader bottleneck at
long sequence lengths.


%%% ----- 7.3.2 Writer Decomposition -----

\subsubsection{Writer Utilization and Stall Decomposition}\label{sec:eval-writer}

Writer-side profiling shows that $cb\_wait$ constitutes 89\% (at
$L{=}$256) to 99.8\% (at $L{=}$32K) of BRISC time.  Source-level
decomposition shows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sender CB wait ($\sim$49.9\%):} Waiting for local
    TRISC to produce partial attention output.
  \item \textbf{Tree-child wait ($\sim$50.1\%):} Waiting for child
    cores to complete their partial results.
\end{itemize}
Root CB wait and output-gather wait are each $<$0.1\%, establishing
that the dominant writer bottleneck is the \emph{pipelined coupling}
between local compute completion and reduction readiness, rather than
final output bandwidth.


%%% ----- 7.3.3 Compute Bubble Analysis -----

\subsubsection{Compute-Pipeline Bubble Analysis}\label{sec:eval-compute}

TRISC idle cycles decompose into \emph{wait-front} (waiting for input)
and \emph{reserve-back} (waiting for output CB space):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Wait-front share decreases from $\sim$87\% (256) to
    $\sim$71\% (32K).
  \item Reserve-back share increases from $\sim$13\% to $\sim$29\%.
\end{itemize}
This indicates that at long sequences, compute transitions from
purely \emph{input-starved} to \emph{squeezed from both ends},
consistent with the reader and writer findings.


%%% ----- 7.3.4 Prefill Comparison -----

\subsubsection{Decode--Prefill Utilization Comparison}\label{sec:eval-prefill}

By contrast, prefill exhibits \emph{reader--writer saturated coupling}
from the shortest tested sequence onward.  PM FPU utilization ranges
from only $\sim$10\% (256) to $\sim$19\% (4K), indicating that the
arithmetic units are not the limiting resource.  This contrast
reinforces our decode-focused optimization strategy.

\subsection{Cost Model Fidelity and \AutoTuner{} Effectiveness}\label{sec:eval-tuner}

We next evaluate whether the calibrated second-order cost model is
sufficiently accurate to guide automated mapping selection, and whether
the resulting \autoTuner{} can identify high-quality configurations with
low search overhead.

%%% ----- 7.4.1 Cost Model Accuracy -----

\subsubsection{Cost Model Fidelity}\label{sec:eval-accuracy}

%% Like FLAT §7's comparison of dataflow variants under different
%% hardware configurations.  Here: comparing model accuracy.

We evaluate the cost model against hardware-measured latencies across
all sequence lengths ($L \in \{256\text{--}32\text{K}\}$) and the
baseline configuration.

\paragraph{First-order model.}
The first-order analytical model (Section~\ref{sec:cm-first}) closely
matches measured latencies at short sequences ($L \leq$ 1K), where
pipeline overlap is nearly ideal.  However, it becomes progressively
optimistic at longer sequences: at $L{=}$16K the prediction is
$\sim$1.4$\times$ lower than measured, and at $L{=}$32K the gap
reaches $\sim$1.8$\times$.  This aligns with Challenge~2
(Section~\ref{sec:challenge-coupling}): the growing pipeline coupling
effects that the first-order model ignores become dominant.

\paragraph{Second-order model.}
After calibrating the three empirical correction terms ($\beta_r,
\beta_w, \beta_m$) using 8 measurement points (one per sequence
length), the second-order model substantially narrows the gap.  The
mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) across all configurations drops
from $\sim$28\% (first-order) to $\sim$8\% (second-order), with the
largest remaining errors at the shortest sequences where correction
terms slightly over-compensate.

\paragraph{Cross-validation.}
To test generalization, we perform leave-one-out cross-validation:
calibrating on 7 sequence lengths and predicting the 8th.  The
second-order model maintains MAPE $<$12\% across all held-out points,
confirming that the linear empirical terms capture the structural
coupling trend rather than over-fitting to individual measurements.

%%% ----- 7.4.2 Auto-Tuner Evaluation -----

\subsubsection{\AutoTuner{} Quality and Efficiency}\label{sec:eval-tuner-auto}

%% Like FLAT's map-space exploration results showing optimal
%% mappings under different constraints.

We then assess whether the \autoTuner{} can identify near-optimal
configurations across the design space.

\paragraph{Recommendation quality.}
For each sequence length, we compare the \autoTuner{}'s top-1
recommendation (scored by the second-order model) against the
empirically best configuration found via exhaustive hardware profiling
of the top-10 model-ranked candidates.  Across all tested sequence
lengths, the \autoTuner{}'s top-1 configuration achieves within
$\sim$5\% of the empirically best latency, indicating that
cost-model-guided ranking is sufficiently faithful for practical tuning.

\paragraph{Regime-dependent trends.}
The recommended configurations exhibit systematic trends that validate
the challenge analysis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item At short sequences ($L \leq$ 1K), the tuner selects high
    $\Ghead$ (more heads per core) and small $\Tgroup$ to maximize
    per-core compute utilization.
  \item At long sequences ($L \geq$ 16K), the tuner shifts to larger
    $\Tgroup$ with deeper pipeline ($D{=}3\text{--}4$) to maximize
    multicast amortization and reduce reader back-pressure.
  \item The transition region (2K--8K) shows the highest sensitivity
    to configuration: multiple configurations achieve similar
    latencies, reflecting the shift between bottleneck regimes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Search overhead.}
The full enumerate--filter--score--rank pipeline evaluates 300--800
feasible configurations in $<$0.8\,s on a single CPU core,
making it practical for configuration selection via the
bucketed policy cache.

\section{Architectural Implications Beyond Wormhole}\label{sec:arch-explore}

We then use the calibrated model to study architectural implications
beyond current Wormhole measurements.  Using the architecture-simulation
flow in Section~\ref{sec:cm-archsim}, we analyze sensitivity to SRAM,
compute throughput, and core granularity, compare MLA and MHA
architectural optima, and project the resulting bottlenecks to future
chips such as Blackhole.


%%% ----- 8.1 SRAM Sensitivity -----

\subsection{SRAM Capacity Sensitivity}\label{sec:arch-sram}

%% Like FLAT §7's on-chip buffer sweep (20KB → 2GB).
%% Here: sweeping L1 SRAM to study impact on pipeline coupling.

To isolate the effect of buffering capacity, we sweep per-core L1 SRAM
from 0.5\,MB to 4\,MB while holding core count, DRAM bandwidth, and
compute throughput constant.  Larger SRAM enables deeper circular
buffers and therefore directly mitigates the K-path reserve stalls
identified in Section~\ref{sec:eval-reader}.

\paragraph{Findings.}
At $L{=}$32K, increasing L1 from 0.5\,MB to 1.5\,MB (the current
Wormhole configuration) reduces decode latency by $\sim$35\%, because
the additional SRAM increases circular-buffer depth from $D{=}1$ to
$D{=}3$.  Increasing L1 further to 4\,MB yields an additional
$\sim$15\% reduction by enabling $D{=}4$ and nearly eliminating K-path
reserve stalls.  By contrast, at shorter sequences ($L \leq$ 2K),
capacities beyond 1.5\,MB provide negligible benefit because pipeline
coupling has not yet become the dominant bottleneck.

\paragraph{Implication.}
These results indicate that the current 1.5\,MB Wormhole L1 is a
reasonable provisioning point for moderate sequence lengths, whereas
long-context MLA decode ($L \geq$ 16K) would justify approximately
$2\times$ larger per-core SRAM.

%%% ----- 8.2 Compute Sensitivity -----

\subsection{Compute Throughput Sensitivity}\label{sec:arch-tops}

%% Like FLAT's analysis of how different HW resources (FLOPs vs BW)
%% affect performance across dataflow variants.

To isolate the role of arithmetic throughput, we sweep per-core compute
throughput from 0.5$\times$ to 4$\times$ the Wormhole baseline while
holding SRAM capacity and DRAM bandwidth constant.

\paragraph{Findings.}
The second-order model indicates that compute exits the critical path
once 7--10 cores are active, largely independent of per-core TOPS.
Doubling per-core compute from 1\,GFLOPS to 2\,GFLOPS reduces decode
latency by only $\sim$8\% at $L{=}$32K (where reader--writer coupling
dominates) but by $\sim$25\% at $L{=}$512 (where compute is critical).
A further 2$\times$ increase to 4\,GFLOPS yields $<$3\% improvement
at $L{=}$32K.

\paragraph{Implication.}
For long-context MLA decode, allocating additional die area to SRAM
capacity and NoC bandwidth yields substantially greater benefit than
increasing per-core compute throughput.  This differs from
compute-bound prefill workloads, for which additional TOPS translates
more directly into throughput, and thus underscores the importance of
workload-specific architectural provisioning.


%%% ----- 8.3 Core Granularity -----

\subsection{Core Granularity Trade-offs}\label{sec:arch-granularity}

%% Like FlatAttention's group-size sweep (4×4 to 32×32).
%% Here: the many-small vs few-large core trade-off.

To examine the trade-off between parallelism and per-core resources, we
compare two isomorphic configurations under the same total die-area
budget: 64 small cores (1.5\,MB L1, $\sim$1\,GFLOPS each) versus
16 large cores (6\,MB L1, $\sim$4\,GFLOPS each).

\paragraph{Many small cores.}
With 64 cores, the multicast fanout per \SblockName{} reaches up to 8
receivers, maximizing DRAM-traffic amortization.  However, the
1.5\,MB L1 limits CB depth, and at $L{=}$32K the K-path reserve
stalls become severe (Section~\ref{sec:eval-reader}).

\paragraph{Few large cores.}
With 16 cores, each core has 6\,MB L1, enabling CB depth $D{=}4$+
and eliminating reserve stalls.  However, the reduced core count
lowers multicast fanout to 2--4 receivers, increasing per-block
DRAM traffic by $\sim$2--4$\times$.

\paragraph{Findings.}
At short sequences ($L \leq$ 2K), 64 small cores outperform by
$\sim$20--30\% due to higher multicast parallelism.  At long
sequences ($L \geq$ 16K), 16 large cores match or surpass the
64-core configuration by $\sim$10--15\%, as the eliminated pipeline
coupling compensates for reduced multicast.  The crossover occurs
near $L \approx$ 8K for the evaluated MLA decode configuration.

%%% ----- 8.4 MLA vs MHA Divergent Optima -----

\subsection{MLA vs.\ MHA: Divergent Architectural Optima}\label{sec:arch-mla-mha}

%% Like FLAT's comparison across models (BERT, TrXL, XLM).
%% Here: comparing across attention variants on the same substrate.

An important architectural question is whether MLA shifts the optimal
design point relative to MHA---analogous to
FLAT's~\cite{kao2023flat} observation that L/A operators require
different dataflow treatment than FC operators.

\paragraph{MHA decode.}
The workload is purely memory-bandwidth bound ($I_{\text{MHA}} =
0.5$~FLOP/Byte).  Consequently, the optimal design prioritizes DRAM
bandwidth: configurations with more DRAM controllers and wider memory
interfaces occupy the Pareto frontier, while SRAM beyond
double-buffering provides negligible benefit.

\paragraph{MLA decode.}
By contrast, the latent projection moves MLA into the compute--memory
regime identified in Section~\ref{sec:challenge-reuse}.  Pipeline
coupling (Section~\ref{sec:challenge-coupling}) in turn makes SRAM
depth and NoC bandwidth first-order parameters.  Under the same
die-area budget, the MLA-optimal configuration allocates $\sim$40\%
more area to per-core SRAM and $\sim$20\% more to NoC links, at the
expense of one fewer DRAM controller than the MHA-optimal design point.

\paragraph{Implication.}
These results suggest that, as latent-compressed attention becomes more
prevalent in next-generation LLMs, accelerator design should adopt
\emph{attention-variant-aware} die-area allocation rather than
optimizing exclusively for the MHA memory-bound regime.  In this
sense, our findings extend FlatAttention's~\cite{flatattention2024}
observation that collective-aware architectures can outperform GPUs on
MHA by showing that MLA further shifts the preferred architectural
balance.


%%% ----- 8.5 Blackhole Projection -----

\subsection{Cross-Generation Projection: Blackhole}\label{sec:arch-bh}

Using the calibrated simulation framework, we project MLA performance
to Tenstorrent Blackhole (Table~\ref{tab:hw-params}), which offers
$\sim$50\% higher DRAM bandwidth and $\sim$10\% more cores than
Wormhole.

\paragraph{First- vs.\ second-order gap.}
The second-order model predicts 1.6--1.8$\times$ higher latency than
the first-order model at $L{=}$16K--32K, consistent with the Wormhole
results.  This indicates that pipeline coupling remains a first-order
consideration for next-generation devices as well; a purely first-order
evaluation would therefore systematically overestimate Blackhole's
advantage over Wormhole.

\paragraph{Alternative dataflow topology.}
Simulating an alternative ``Method~B'' topology (native Flash-MLA
layout with 8 DRAM banks) on Blackhole predicts 3.1--5.6$\times$
speedup at 32K tokens relative to the Wormhole-migrated layout,
primarily owing to better DRAM-bank utilization and reduced NoC
contention with 8 rather than 6 memory controllers.

\paragraph{Active-core scaling.}
Sweeping the number of active cores on Blackhole shows that performance
saturates at approximately 48--56 cores for $L{=}$32K.  Beyond this
point, additional cores increase multicast fanout but do not further
alleviate the reader--writer coupling bottleneck.  This observation is
consistent with the compute-throughput analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:arch-tops}) and provides a concrete active-core
target for power-optimized deployment.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}


%%% ----- 9.1 Attention Dataflow on Accelerators -----

\subsection{Attention Dataflow Optimization}\label{sec:rw-attn}

\paragraph{GPU-based approaches.}
FlashAttention~\cite{dao2022flashattention} introduced IO-aware tiling
for attention on GPUs, followed by
FlashAttention-2~\cite{dao2023flashattention2} with improved
parallelism and FlashAttention-3~\cite{shah2024flashattention3}
exploiting Hopper asynchrony.
For decode, Flash-Decoding~\cite{flashdecoding2023} parallelizes
across the KV sequence dimension, and
FlashDecoding++~\cite{hong2024flashdecoding} eliminates synchronization
overhead.  Ring Attention~\cite{liu2023ringattention} distributes long
sequences across devices.  These techniques are intrinsically tied to
the GPU execution model and do not transfer to spatial accelerators.

\paragraph{Attention on many-core architectures.}
FLAT~\cite{kao2023flat} pioneered a specialized inter-operator dataflow
for attention's activation-activation operators (L and A), fusing them
with a tiling approach that transforms intermediate tensor growth from
$\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to linear.  FLAT's map-space exploration framework
directly inspires our \autoTuner{} design.
FlatAttention~\cite{flatattention2024} co-optimizes dataflow and
on-chip fabric collectives (multicast, reduction) for MHA on tile-based
many-PE accelerators, achieving up to 89\% utilization by leveraging
tile groups with row/column-wise multicast and reduction.  Our
\SblockName{} sender--receiver structure and tree reduction are conceptually
related to FlatAttention's group multicast and row-wise reduction, but
we target MLA's distinct shared-latent structure and decode-specific
characteristics.

Neither FLAT nor FlatAttention addresses MLA's latent restoration,
decode-phase pipeline coupling, or automated DSE on Tensix-class
decoupled architectures.


%%% ----- 9.2 Dataflow Frameworks -----

\subsection{Dataflow Mapping Frameworks}\label{sec:rw-framework}

Timeloop~\cite{parashar2019timeloop},
MAESTRO~\cite{kwon2019maestro}, and Eyeriss\,v2~\cite{chen2019eyerissv2}
formalize DNN accelerator dataflow mapping but primarily target
convolution and do not model MLA's latent restoration or
spatial-accelerator-specific features (hardware multicast, decoupled
pipelines, circular buffers).


%%% ----- 9.3 KV Cache and MLA -----

\subsection{KV Cache Management and MLA Implementations}\label{sec:rw-kv}

PagedAttention~\cite{kwon2023pagedattention} in vLLM addresses cache
fragmentation via virtual-memory paging.  FlexGen~\cite{sheng2023flexgen},
Orca~\cite{yu2022orca}, DeepSpeed-Inference~\cite{aminabadi2022deepspeed},
Splitwise~\cite{patel2024splitwise}, and
DistServe~\cite{zhong2024distserve} target system-level memory
placement, batching, or prefill/decode scheduling.
GQA~\cite{ainslie2023gqa}, MQA~\cite{shazeer2019mqa},
StreamingLLM~\cite{xiao2024streamingllm}, and
H\textsubscript{2}O~\cite{zhang2024h2o} reduce or compress KV data.
These are orthogonal to dataflow design.
MLA itself originates in DeepSeek-V2/V3~\cite{deepseekai2024deepseekv2,
deepseekai2024deepseekv3}.  FlashMLA~\cite{flashmla2024gpu} implements a GPU-optimized kernel
for MLA decode but does not address spatial-accelerator dataflow or DSE.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
To our knowledge, no prior work jointly addresses (i)~MLA-specific
dataflow with latent-restoration awareness, (ii)~second-order pipeline
coupling modeling on spatial accelerators, and (iii)~automated DSE
with hardware-calibrated cost models.
\sysname fills this gap, building on prior attention-dataflow and
collective-aware accelerator analyses while targeting the unique
characteristics of MLA on decoupled spatial architectures.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \sysname, a framework for analyzing and exploring MLA
dataflows on spatial accelerators.  Guided by the three core mapping
challenges, and combining systematic characterization, second-order cost
modeling, current-hardware evaluation on Tenstorrent Wormhole, and
architecture-level projection, we establish four principal findings:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item MLA's shared-latent structure creates data reuse opportunities
    (multicast) absent in MHA, but its online restoration adds
    computational complexity that places it in a mixed
    compute--memory regime, making dataflow design the primary
    performance lever.

  \item Current-hardware evaluation on Wormhole reveals a clear
    bottleneck \phaseTransition{} in decode, from compute-critical at
    short sequences to reader--writer saturated coupling at long
    sequences.  Microarchitectural profiling attributes this transition
    to K-path circular-buffer turnover on the reader side, coupled
    writer waiting, and increasing compute squeeze from both input and
    output boundaries.

  \item The calibrated second-order cost model substantially improves
    predictive fidelity over the first-order model, reducing MAPE from
    $\sim$28\% to $\sim$8\%, while enabling an \autoTuner{} whose top-1
    recommendation remains within $\sim$5\% of the empirically best
    measured configuration.

  \item Architectural exploration beyond Wormhole shows that MLA favors
    a different resource balance than MHA on the same spatial
    substrate: deeper per-core SRAM and stronger on-chip communication
    are more valuable than additional compute throughput, and
    cross-generation projection indicates that both topology choice and
    active-core provisioning remain critical on future devices such as
    Blackhole.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Future work.}
Promising directions include implementing and validating the
optimization opportunities identified by our profiling, including
K-path deepening, BRISC offloading, and streaming tree reduction.
Additional extensions include broadening the cost model to cover
prefill, longer-context regimes, and multi-card sequence-parallel
execution, as well as integrating the \autoTuner{} with TT-NN's
compilation pipeline for automatic dataflow selection.
